FITZWILLIAM DARCY: A CHARACTER ANALYSIS

Pride is Darcy's wound and his armor. Austen introduces him at the Meryton ball as "prouder than anybody," and the first impression is accurate but incomplete: his pride is not vanity craving admiration, it is a habit of judgment, the reflex of a man worth ten thousand a year who has never needed to be agreeable.

The proposal at Hunsford is the hinge. He insults Elizabeth's family while declaring his love, and she rejects him for exactly the right reasons: he separated Bingley from Jane on class grounds, and he buried Wickham's character under the family shame he would not explain. The letter that follows is Austen's quiet masterstroke, a proud man choosing to be accurate about himself when lying would be easier. Darcy does not change his standards, he corrects his facts. He was wrong about Wickham's case, not wrong about judging, and Elizabeth's "till this moment I never knew myself" is the novel's thesis arriving through him first.

What makes him durable is that his love never asks Elizabeth to shrink. He does not convert her to his circle, he reorganizes his own world to admit hers. The reconciliation is two people who each did the painful homework, which is why the ending feels earned rather than granted.

Austen's trick: the whole novel is filtered through Elizabeth's wit, so Darcy's arc happens almost entirely offstage, in small actions, paying for Wickham's marriage, retrieving Lydia, swallowing his pride at Pemberley's gate. He is what the reader assembles from evidence, which is exactly how Elizabeth must read him, and how she finally reads herself.
